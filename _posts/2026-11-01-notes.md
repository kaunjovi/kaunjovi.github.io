Anthropic just published a detailed architecture guide for always-on agents — systems that wake on events, carry memory across sessions, and act without constant user input 🧩

The five primitives they outline:

📥 Event inbox — a durable queue so nothing's lost when events arrive simultaneously
⏱️ Timers — follow-ups and schedules that wake the agent proactively
🧠 Memory — user instructions, notes, task progress, past messages persisted across runs
🔐 Permissions — credential proxies, auto-mode classifiers, deferred tool calls for human review
📤 Outbox — controlled message delivery so agents don't flood users

What stands out: the model itself is one component. The surrounding architecture — event handling, state management, permission layers — is where the real engineering lives.

And it's where the cost and risk live too. Each primitive needs its own durable store, its own failure handling, its own tuning. Anthropic even flags that getting message frequency right requires building evals.

## [Data Ontology: Definition, Examples, and Palantir's Model](https://upsolve.ai/blog/data-ontology)
   
1. Palantir says its Ontology is “not a ‘semantic layer’.” 
2. Microsoft says its new ontology “provides a common context layer.” 
3. Databricks calls it “the context layer your AI agents are missing.”

4. **A data ontology models meaning, not storage**: It defines the things your business cares about, their properties, how they relate, and the rules that apply, independent of how tables are laid out.
5. **Ontology + data = knowledge graph**: The ontology is the blueprint. The knowledge graph is the blueprint filled in with real instances.
6. There are two kinds of ontology in circulation: 
7. **Formal ontologies (OWL, RDF)** are built for reasoning. 
8. **Operational ontologies (Palantir, Microsoft Fabric IQ, Databricks Genie)** bind business objects to live data, permissions, and actions.


## What is Data Ontology 
1. https://upsolve.ai/blog/data-ontology
2. A data ontology is a formal, shared model of the things a business cares about (entities such as customers, contracts, and invoices), their attributes, how they relate to each other, and the rules that constrain them. 
3. It describes what data means, independently of how it’s stored, so people and machines interpret it the same way.
4. It is the agreed-upon answer to “what is a customer here, and how does it connect to everything else?”. Who agreed?? Where is the agreement documented?? 
5. **Data Model**
   1. A data model describes storage: tables, columns, data types, and foreign keys. 
   2. An ontology describes meaning: a Customer holds Subscriptions, an Invoice is billed to exactly one Customer, and an Active Customer is one with at least one live subscription. 
   3. As Databricks puts it, “A schema describes how data is structured. An ontology describes what that data means in the context of the business.” 
   4. You can change the data model (split a table, rename a column) without changing the ontology.

## [A Benchmark to Understand the Role of Knowledge Graphs on Large Language Model's Accuracy for Question Answering on Enterprise SQL Databases](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07509)

1. Enterprise applications of Large Language Models (LLMs) hold promise for question answering on enterprise SQL databases. 
2. However, the extent to which LLMs can accurately respond to enterprise questions in such databases remains unclear, given the absence of suitable Text-to-SQL benchmarks tailored to enterprise settings. 
3. Additionally, the potential of Knowledge Graphs (KGs) to enhance LLM-based question answering by providing business context is not well understood. 
4. This study aims to evaluate the accuracy of LLM-powered question answering systems in the context of enterprise questions and SQL databases, while also exploring the role of knowledge graphs in improving accuracy. 
5. To achieve this, we introduce a benchmark comprising an enterprise SQL schema in the insurance domain, a range of enterprise queries encompassing reporting to metrics, and a contextual layer incorporating an ontology and mappings that define a knowledge graph. Our primary finding reveals that question answering using GPT-4, with zero-shot prompts directly on SQL databases, achieves an accuracy of 16%. Notably, this accuracy increases to 54% when questions are posed over a Knowledge Graph representation of the enterprise SQL database.
